# Contribution

The contribution is a reusable procedure by which a language-model agent converts an owned VulnHub-style OVA into a Docker Compose replica, and a fixed evaluation that says whether that conversion is complete.

The procedure is six skills, applied in precondition order. The agent imports one OVA onto an existing NAT network, recovers local administration only from a powered-off disk clone, repairs end-of-life apt without trusting a mirror that is still current, inventories the guest without printing secrets, builds one container when the database is local, and scores the replica against the inventory. Each skill is written for the next appliance, not for one guest name. The agent is expected to open the skill that matches the current precondition, follow the checklist, and stop at the stated conditions. When a trap is discovered during a conversion, the important part is written back into the skill so the next agent does not pay for the same discovery. That is the difference from an agent that plans only inside one chat (Yao et al., 2023). The checklist form follows Haynes et al. (2009): required actions and pause points, not a narrative of one session.

The evaluation is the other half of the contribution. Eleven rows are scored pass, partial, or missing: listening ports, unpublished localhost-only services, package versions, users, sudoers, virtual hosts, web paths, databases, cron, mail, and the application URL. Partial means the item exists with a stated substitution. Missing means the item is absent, or a localhost-only guest service was published. Archive apt, the shared kernel, and the process supervisor stay in an explicit gap list even when the eleven rows pass. A report that prints a password, a hash, or a flag value fails even if the application URL answers. That outcome definition is stricter than "the page loaded," and it is the check Jimenez et al. (2024) argue a software-agent result needs: a completed result under a test, not a plausible story. Felter et al. (2015) and Naik (2016) explain why a smaller replica is attractive. They do not supply this score. Boettiger (2015) explains why the kernel gap is structural. Nüst et al. (2020) explain why the package row is about named revisions.

The with-and-without comparison follows from that pairing. With the skills, the agent is given the order, the legal repairs, the verification commands, and the rows it must not promote. Without the skills, the agent has the guest and, after the fact, a Dockerfile that records chosen settings but not the rules that made those settings mandatory. On this appliance the difference showed up as quality of the replica, not as a measured model latency. An earlier replica still substituted Debian 9 and MariaDB and left listeners, cron, mail, and the application URL partial. After the procedure was applied and the new traps were written into the skills, the parity report marked all eleven rows pass and kept the three gaps. A second agent was not timed, so the paper does not claim a speedup in seconds of model time. It does claim that the skills are where the decision rules live, and that the evaluation result is the parity report rather than the existence of a container.

The paper does not contribute an attack procedure, a new runtime, or a general verifier in the sense of Shambaugh et al. (2016).
